### Language-rule finding

I found a **clear motive/design attribution** in the public wording: F-0007 says the commerce subsystem was off-chain "by design," and the Nov-2008 artifact calls the object a "developer's test fixture." Those are not merely observations of bytes or execution; they attribute purpose.

I also found **categorical language** inconsistent with a literal "no absolutes" rule, especially the `STILL TRUE` / `FALSE` formulations and the "every vector" construction.

I did **not** substantiate a jurisdictional-language violation from the public material I was able to retrieve. I would not invent one.

### What the record does not say

The strongest unanswered objection is the **network-consensus inference problem**. The record is capable of showing that a particular January 2009 binary, a later release, or a laboratory reconstruction behaves in a particular way. It is much less capable of showing that the wider 2009 Bitcoin network actually enforced that behaviour. Two nodes running the same binary are not independent consensus witnesses; a model reproducing a rule is not a historical peer; and a later Bitcoin commit proves what later code did, not by itself what contemporaneous independent nodes accepted. The constitution itself concedes several historically decisive execution witnesses remain `open`, including the 1 MB narrowing, sigop rule, transaction-size transition, DER acceptance on the 2009 binary, and numeric operand cap.

**Single weakest point:** the laboratory's weakest evidentiary bridge is the jump from **software archaeology to historical network consensus**. Its strongest artifacts can establish source contents and reproducible behaviour, but the public record does not supply an independent contemporaneous consensus witness for the proposition that the reconstructed behaviour was actually the rule enforced by the 2009 network. That is the central vulnerability; the remaining defects are mostly local manifestations of it.
